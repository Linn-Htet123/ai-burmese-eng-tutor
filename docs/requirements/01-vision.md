## The problem

There is a large group of Burmese adults who are not beginners. They read English, they write English, some of them work in English every day. They are not failing at English. They are failing at *speaking* it in real time.

The specific failure looks like this. You know exactly what you want to say. You form the thought in Burmese. You start translating. The translation takes two seconds too long. By the time you have the sentence, the conversation has moved on and someone else is talking. You said nothing, or you said a smaller, safer version of what you meant.

That is not a knowledge problem. It is a **retrieval speed** problem. The vocabulary is in there. The grammar is mostly in there. What is missing is the path from intention to spoken English that runs fast enough to hold a turn in a live conversation.

This distinction matters because it determines the entire product. Knowledge problems respond to lessons. Retrieval problems respond only to repetition under time pressure. Every grammar course sold to this person is solving the wrong problem, which is why they have bought several and still stall.

A second, related problem: **situations without a script.** A first job interview in English. Pitching to a foreign company. A performance review. The learner does not know what the shape of that conversation is supposed to be, in any language, and is trying to solve the language and the situation simultaneously. Nobody teaches the situation.

### Why the existing options do not fix it

* **Grammar apps and courses.** Recognition practice. You tap the right answer. You never speak under pressure. Solves a problem this user does not have.
* **YouTube and free content.** Zero production. Zero feedback. Consumption feels like progress and is not.
* **Human tutors (italki, Preply, local).** Actually work. Cost $8 to $25 an hour, need scheduling, and most Burmese adults will not book a stranger and speak badly in front of them. The embarrassment barrier is real and underrated.
* **Conversation apps.** Let you talk. Then say "great job!" and move on. They correct errors when there are errors. This user makes few outright errors. Their English is not wrong, it is just not smooth, and nothing on the market addresses the gap between correct and natural.

## What we are building

A voice tutoring product for spoken English, built around a tutor character called **May**, that upgrades the way you already speak through **daily 40-minute practice on a monthly subscription**. The first month has a pack-shaped arc (interview-ready in 4 weeks) that ends with a before/after clip — the retention moment (D-042, D-043).

Open a page, press one button, and you are in a live spoken lesson with a tutor who already knows your level, your goal, and what you got wrong last time. No booking, no waiting, no scheduling.

Five things define it.

### 1. The upgrade loop

The core interaction, and the thing that does not exist elsewhere.

The learner says a sentence. May confirms it works, then gives the smoother, more natural version. The learner says the better version back, out loud.

Not correction. **Upgrading.** The difference is the entire product positioning. Correction says you were wrong. Upgrading says you were fine, here is better. For an adult who already speaks English and is sensitive about how they sound, that framing is the difference between using the product and quietly deleting it.

The learner repeating the better version aloud is not optional politeness. It is the mechanism. Hearing a better sentence changes nothing. Saying it is what lays down the retrieval path.

### 2. Daily practice with a pack-shaped first month

A monthly subscription at $25/month, one 40-minute session per day cap (D-042). But marketing surfaces the **pack** — the first-month arc, interview-ready in 4 weeks — because "daily English practice" is generic and "interview-ready in 4 weeks" is not.

Adults quit things that run forever and finish things that end. A pure subscription with no endpoint has no moment where quitting feels like failure. The **pack-shaped first month** carries the same commitment psychology as a course: a real event, a specific outcome, a week-4 before/after clip that says "you did it" — and only *then* does the subscription mechanic take over to keep the daily habit going into month 2 (D-042, D-043).

Interviews specifically, because the deadline is real, money is attached, people already spend money on interview preparation, and it is the easiest thing to write an advertisement for. Interviews is only pack #1 (D-043); profession packs — nurses, drivers, shop owners, teachers — are the roadmap that turns month-1 completion into month-6 retention.

### 3. It teaches the situation, not only the language

The course is not "English for interviews" as a vocabulary list. It is: here is what an interview actually sounds like, here is the shape of a good answer, here is how you buy yourself three seconds when you need to think, and here is how to say your own real answer smoothly.

### 4. Levels, and they change what a session is

The learner picks a level from 0 to 10, and it does more than adjust difficulty. At level 0 the session is a conversation partner plus explicit grammar teaching, mostly in Burmese. At level 10 it is almost pure phrasing work: register, precision, how to say something well. The stages themselves change, not just the vocabulary.

This is why the product serves a market rather than one person. Grammar is taught where the level calls for it, always through examples before rules, and always selected by what that learner actually got wrong rather than by curriculum order.

### 5. It practises what you actually care about

Two mechanisms, kept separate on purpose. A **goal pack** is authored content for a situation, interviews first. An **interest** is just something the learner tells us they like, and it supplies the conversation material. Someone preparing for interviews who likes football gets interview structure discussed through football and through their own real work.

Packs are expensive to build. Interests are nearly free. That split is what makes personalisation affordable for a solo founder.

## The wedge

Job interview preparation for Burmese adults who already speak some English.

Narrow on purpose. One profession, one first-month pack arc, one clear buying trigger. Everything else — standups, client calls, presentations, negotiations, general fluency, and other professions (nurses, drivers, shop owners, teachers, per D-043) — is a later pack built on the same engine, unlocked as monthly subscribers stay past month 1.

The wedge is chosen for sellability, not for size. It is the easiest version of this product to explain in one sentence to a stranger on Facebook.

## What success looks like

**Twelve months.** Several hundred people have completed the first-month pack arc. A meaningful share have kept renewing month-over-month. There is a library of before and after clips, in learners' own voices, that sells the product better than any copy. Profession packs beyond interviews (D-043) exist.

**The proof that matters.** A learner's week-one recording next to their week-four recording. Same person, same question, visibly and audibly smoother. That single artefact is the marketing, the retention tool, and the investor demo.

**The honest version.** This becomes a real business when adults renew and tell other adults. If month-1 pack completion is low, none of the rest matters — and if completion is high but month-2 renewal is low, the subscription model is wrong. Both are the untested bets the whole thing rests on (D-042).

## What we are explicitly not building

* A grammar *curriculum*. Grammar is taught, but driven by the learner's own errors, never as a syllabus to march through
* An accent reduction product. Slow to move, rarely the thing that actually breaks communication, and fixing it costs far more than fixing vocabulary and retrieval speed
* A chatbot with no structure. Open-ended conversation practice is the thing that feels productive and is not
* An absolute-beginner product. Someone with no English at all is a different product and a different price. Level 0 means "has words, cannot reliably form sentences", not "has no English"
* A teacher-facing or employer-facing dashboard, at this stage
* A general purpose "learn English" app. General is why the existing ones do not work
